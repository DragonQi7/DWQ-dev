The Qi Brothers Collection

A private library assembled around two Chinese scholar-official brothers—not a single artwork, not a painting, and not a set of books they personally wrote.

In plain English: it is a named group of historical materials that once belonged to the Qi family and is now preserved at the Harvard-Yenching Library, 2 Divinity Avenue, Cambridge, Massachusetts, USA.
Official recordstaxes · law · administration
Handwritten booksclassics · history · literature

What survived

One collection, many kinds of evidence

It is

  • a private family library kept together as a named archival collection;
  • mostly manuscripts, copied books, working records, scores, and maps;
  • a window into government, commerce, education, music, and daily life.

It is not

  • one painting or one illustrated album;
  • a collection authored entirely by the two brothers;
  • a perfectly documented library—the article says its precise origins remain uncertain.

A second family-history source

What the Yitong Qi Family Genealogy tells us

A 2005 article by Zhang Wanyou describes a privately preserved family register titled Yitong Qi Family Genealogy. It is a handwritten, right-bound book—not a painting—and records seven generations of the family.

“Qi the Eighth Hanlin” explained

This was Qi Zhongjia’s nickname. He served in the Hanlin Academy and was the eighth-ranking son in his family. The phrase does not mean that there were eight Hanlin scholars.

1 bookabout 26.5 × 19.5 cm, with a dark-green cover
7 generationslinked through names, relationships, dates, education, careers, and residences
4 jinshiuncle-and-nephew relatives who passed the highest imperial examination from 1890 to 1895

How the family record is organized

PrefaceExplains why and when the genealogy was compiled and revised.
Lineage chartConnects ancestors and descendants generation by generation.
Family registerLists names, sex, birth information, education, work, office, and residence.
BiographiesRecords notable relatives’ names, offices, character, and major achievements.

The four examination graduates highlighted in the article

The source describes them as two men of the older generation and two nephews of the younger generation. This is a family cluster, not a complete lineage chart.

A culture of educationThe article portrays the family as deeply committed to study, teaching, local schools, and helping students—including some from poor families.
Women were recordedUnlike many traditional genealogies that recorded only men, this one included daughters’ birth dates, education, marriages, spouses, and spouses’ occupations.
Names show generationsLater generations often shared a generation character, such as Yao (耀) or Zong (宗), helping readers identify a person’s place in the family.

Use with care: Zhang treats the genealogy as valuable evidence for descendants tracing their roots and for correcting errors in local histories. At the same time, it is a family-produced source with a strongly admiring tone. It contains no portraits, omits the clan rules found in many genealogies, and should be compared with official records and other independent evidence.

Source summarized in clear English: Zhang Wanyou, “A Study of the Genealogical Archives of ‘Qi the Eighth Hanlin’” (2005), supplied pages 56–57. The unrelated archive-management article printed on the lower half of the second scan is not part of the Qi-family study.

A political crisis in Qi Yaolin’s career

Why the Jiangsu Provincial Assembly impeached him

In 1920, Jiangsu’s elected provincial assembly accused Governor Qi Yaolin of mishandling appointments, finances, and the assembly’s lawful decisions. The historian Zhu Ying argues that the charges had real substance—but that personal interests and political rivalry also intensified the campaign.

1920

What “impeachment” meant here: the assembly formally accused the provincial governor of unlawful conduct and asked the central government in Beijing to investigate and punish him. The assembly could accuse him, but it could not remove him by itself.

Jiangsu Provincial AssemblyClaimed a legal duty to oversee provincial administration, budgets, property, and officials.
Who had the final authority?
Governor Qi YaolinControlled the provincial administration and repeatedly resisted, delayed, or reinterpreted assembly decisions.

The assembly’s four main accusations

1Changing or ignoring assembly decisionsIt said Qi altered approved budgets, failed to carry out resolutions, and improperly handled provincial property.
2Tolerating gifts and misconductIt accused him of accepting birthday gifts from subordinates and failing to investigate senior officials credibly accused of improper behavior.
3Protecting poorly performing officialsThe assembly said officials accused of abuse or corruption were shielded, rewarded, or transferred instead of being disciplined.
4Weak financial accountabilityIt alleged that county officials were not required to complete proper handovers of tax accounts and public funds.

How the confrontation escalated

JuneMore than two-thirds of voting members approved an impeachment proposal against Qi.
AugustNinety-five members requested an emergency session after Qi appointed the controversial Yu Jiqi as finance director.
August–OctoberQi refused the emergency meeting. The dispute expanded into a constitutional argument over the assembly’s right to convene.
AutumnThe assembly passed another impeachment measure. Beijing sent an investigator, and Qi’s resignation was accepted.
DecemberAfter his resignation was announced, silk workers stormed the assembly and assaulted or abducted members, deepening the controversy over official responsibility.

Why the criticism was credible

The article finds substantial evidence that Qi’s personnel choices, administrative decisions, and treatment of assembly resolutions helped create the crisis. His resistance also weakened the assembly’s authority.

Why the story is more complicated

Some legislators had unsuccessfully sought higher salaries and may have used impeachment partly to settle personal grievances. Nearly one-third did not support the first measure, and some later accusations lacked firm evidence.

The author’s balanced conclusion: Republican China had created provincial legislatures on paper, but they lacked stable authority and were limited by factionalism and uneven political practice. Even so, the Jiangsu and Zhejiang assemblies did exert real pressure: both Qi brothers ultimately left their governorships after impeachment campaigns.

Source summarized in clear English: Zhu Ying, “Conflict Between Provincial Assemblies and Governors during the Republican Period: The Jiangsu Provincial Assembly’s Impeachment of the Governor as a Case Study” (2007).

The article’s central argument

Ordinary-looking records can become extraordinary evidence.

As luxury rare books

Many copies are rough, practical, and below the traditional standard of a fine or prestigious “rare edition.”

As historical documents

They preserve how local government, commerce, schools, music, and daily work actually functioned—often in sources found nowhere else.

Why scholars care

Six kinds of history preserved

Biography and relationships
Evidence about the Qi brothers and the networks surrounding late-Qing and early-Republican officials.
Politics and institutions
Records of government, finance, military affairs, law, salt administration, and modernization.
Everyday social life
Documents involving scholars, farmers, artisans, merchants, schools, temples, and businesses.
Individual lives
Traces of officials’ self-education, clerks’ training, and ordinary students’ academy experience.
Literature and art
Unpublished poetry, dramatic scripts, musical notation, calligraphy, painting, and refined amusements.
China and the West
The collection’s journey abroad records a larger twentieth-century story of war, loss, preservation, and cultural exchange.

A Brief Account of the Former Collection of the Two Qi Brothers at the Harvard-Yenching Library

Le Yi

Page 14

Among the Harvard-Yenching Library’s Chinese holdings are rare books of considerable value for the study of editions, as well as special collections that came from individual scholars and private collectors. Each of these collections forms a coherent body of its own. Because they combine their collectors’ scholarly insights with the historical circumstances of their time, they possess distinctive documentary value and deserve scholarly attention. The former collection of the brothers Qi Yaolin and Qi Yaoshan—hereafter, the “Qi Brothers Collection”—is one such body of material.¹

I

The Harvard-Yenching Library’s accession records show that the Qi Brothers Collection entered the library in June 1945. A review of the papers of Kaiming Chiu, the library’s first director, together with the Harvard-Yenching Library’s annual reports, reveals that the collection’s acquisition followed a rather circuitous course.

From the outset of his tenure, Chiu displayed a discerning eye in purchasing books. In 1933, when he returned to Beiping and helped Yenching University establish its library, he was already attentive to acquiring private libraries.³ He received the support of the dean of Harvard’s Faculty of Arts and Sciences,⁴ because at that time the Chinese-Japanese Library of the Harvard-Yenching Institute was jointly administered by the Faculty of Arts and Sciences and the Harvard University Library.

Notes

  1. Hereafter abbreviated as the “Qi Brothers Collection.”
  2. Kaiming Chiu (Alfred Kaiming Chiu, 1898–1977), a native of Zhenhai, Zhejiang, graduated from the Boone Library School. In 1924 he went to the United States for advanced study in library science and economics. He served as director of the Harvard-Yenching Library from 1931 to 1965.
  3. Chiu wrote to George Henry Chase: “As for the purchase of books, I am working with the head of acquisitions at the Yenching University Library. I plan first to purchase some of the most important works—such as local gazetteers from the southern provinces, books on archaeology and art, encyclopedias, biographies, and so forth—to strengthen our holdings. If private collections do not contain too many duplicate copies, we may have an opportunity to acquire them.” (HYL Archives, “Letter of Alfred K’aiming Ch’iu to George Henry Chase,” November 15, 1930; Cheng Huanwen, ed., Chronicle of Kaiming Chiu, Guangxi Normal University Press, October 2008, p. 49.)
  4. George Henry Chase wrote to Chiu: “Your letter of November 15 arrived before winter. What you report about the purchase of books is good news to me. I am convinced that we have a great need for local gazetteers and books on archaeology, art, biography, and so forth, and that private libraries should be purchased, provided they do not supply too many duplicates.” (HYL Archives, “Letters of George Henry Chase to Alfred K’aiming Ch’iu,” December 23, 1930; Cheng Huanwen, Chronicle of Kaiming Chiu, p. 50.)

Page 15

In July 1934, Professor Serge Elisséeff was formally appointed director of the Harvard-Yenching Institute and professor of Far Eastern languages at Harvard University. That same year, the relevant administrative system was reformed, and the director of the Chinese-Japanese Library came under the direct leadership of the Institute’s director. Through the joint efforts of Elisséeff and Kaiming Chiu, together with the strong support of the Institute’s board, the Harvard-Yenching Library’s holdings of Chinese books—including Manchu- and Tibetan-language works—as well as Japanese and Korean materials increased markedly. In China during the early 1930s, the clouds of war hung overhead and society was in turmoil. Many old and prominent families dispersed their collections of ancient books, creating opportunities for Chiu to acquire rare editions.

In April 1944, Chiu received a letter from Felton Chow in Washington and encountered, for the first time, a list of books from the Qi Brothers Collection. According to the letter, Wang Wenshan, who had worked in China’s Ministry of Communications, had asked Chow to forward the list to Chiu and inquire whether the Harvard-Yenching Library might be interested in purchasing it. The books had been taken out of China by Wang roughly ten years earlier and deposited at the Library of Congress.¹

Notes

  1. Elisséeff’s strong leadership laid a firm foundation for the Harvard-Yenching Institute, and the Harvard Chinese-Japanese Library immediately entered a period of growth. After he took office, its Western-language, Tibetan, Manchu, Mongolian, and later Korean collections were established one after another. During the eight years from July 1, 1934, to July 1, 1942, the Chinese-Japanese Library’s holdings doubled. (Alfred K’aiming Ch’iu, “The Harvard-Yenching Institute Library,” The Far Eastern Quarterly, vol. 14, no. 1 [November 1954], pp. 147–152; Cheng Huanwen, Chronicle of Kaiming Chiu, p. 115.)
  2. After Professor Serge Elisséeff became director of the Harvard-Yenching Institute, the Chinese-Japanese Library’s administrative structure changed. Its director was no longer subject to the dual leadership of the Harvard University Librarian and the dean of the Faculty of Arts and Sciences, but reported directly to the Institute’s director and was responsible for managing and developing the library. (HYL Archives, Chinese-Japanese Library of the Harvard-Yenching Institute at Harvard University: Report of the Librarian for 1963–1964, p. 39.) With support from the trustees and Chiu’s professional discernment, the library began purchasing the best available editions of Chinese books. Chinese books were then inexpensive in comparison with Japanese materials, and many good editions had appeared on the market because of warfare. The library took full advantage of this opportunity. Its holdings consequently increased. (Serge Elisséeff, “The Chinese-Japanese Library of the Harvard-Yenching Institute,” Harvard Library Bulletin, vol. 10, no. 1 [Winter 1956], pp. 73–97; the foregoing is cited in Cheng Huanwen, Chronicle of Kaiming Chiu, pp. 115–116.)
  3. Felton Chow of China Defense Supplies, Inc., Washington, wrote to Chiu: “When I left Chongqing in December 1943, Wang Wenshan of the Ministry of Communications gave me a book list. I knew that he wanted you to deal with the books in the United States, but he gave me no definite instructions. Wang has just contacted me and asks that you reply as to whether you would be willing to purchase the books on the list. When convenient, please let me know, and I can forward your letter to Wang.” (HYL Archives, “Letters of Felton Chow to Alfred Kaiming Ch’iu,” April 21, 1944; Cheng Huanwen, Chronicle of Kaiming Chiu, p. 302.)
  4. Wang Wenshan (1901–?), a native of Hanchuan, Hubei, graduated from the library-science department of Boone University in Wuchang in 1923 and then became head of the library at Nankai University in Tianjin. He went to the United States in the winter of 1926 and earned a master’s degree in library science from Columbia University and master’s and doctoral degrees in political science from the University of Washington. He served for a time as China’s chargé d’affaires in the United States and, while in America, worked as a cataloger in the Chinese section of the Library of Congress. He returned to China in June 1932 and became director of the Tsinghua University Library. In October 1933 he became a commissioner of the National Defense Council in Nanjing. In December 1935 he became secretary-general of the National Resources Commission and concurrently director of its library. In July 1938 he became a division chief in the National Trade Commission; in early 1939 he transferred to the Ministry of Communications as director of its Personnel Department. He left the ministry in December 1943 to take a post with the China Industrial Service Association. (Based on Zhang Hongwei, “Remembering Wang Wenshan, a Second-Class Graduate of Boone’s Library Science Program,” Library and Information Knowledge, 2010, no. 4, pp. 125–128, and Yuan Jin, “National Government Gazette—Orders.”)

Judging from Wang Wenshan’s career, he went to the United States to study in the winter of 1926 and worked there as a cataloger in the Chinese section of the Library of Congress. It may therefore be inferred that he took the Qi Brothers Collection out of China when he went abroad—sometime between 1926 and 1932—and deposited it at the Library of Congress.

After receiving the letter and book list, Chiu consulted Elisséeff. Elisséeff sent the list to Yuan Tongli, then director of the National Library of Peiping, and asked for his opinion. At the time, Wang Zhongmin had been assigned by that library to help organize Chinese rare books at the Library of Congress. He therefore examined Wang Wenshan’s books and assessed them. He judged the collection to have some value and wrote to Chiu in February 1945 explaining the situation.¹

The Qi family then used Wang Wenshan as intermediary and also retained Liu Yuwan of the China Institute of Pacific Relations² to handle, with full authority, the sale of the collection as a whole to the Harvard-Yenching Library. The library, in turn, appointed Kaiming Chiu as its fully authorized representative. The parties reached agreement, and the entire acquisition was completed in March 1945.³ In June 1945, the Library of Congress transferred the Qi Brothers Collection as a whole to the Harvard-Yenching Library. In his library report of October 1945, Chiu reviewed the acquisition in detail and briefly introduced the collection’s distinctive features and several representative works.

According to Harvard-Yenching Library records, a short inventory was made when the collection entered the library. The handwritten list recorded more than five hundred works and used a simple system of classification and grading: “A,” “AA,” and “AAA” distinguished the quality of an edition; “M” signified a map; and “B” indicated that a book’s author was a government official. In 2006, the Harvard-Yenching Library reinventoried and reorganized the collection, removed items that could no longer be located, and consolidated certain entries. The final catalog contained 413 works in 1,206 volumes, all of which were converted into machine-readable records and entered into the Harvard University Library’s public catalog.

II

Qi Yaolin and Qi Yaoshan were born during the Tongzhi reign of the Qing and were full brothers, Yaolin being the elder. Both passed the jinshi metropolitan examination toward the end of the Qing and served as officials in different regions. After the Republic was founded, their respective ties to Yuan Shikai and Li Yuanhong led to their appointment in the new government. No complete biographical account of the Qi brothers survives, and no previous scholar has systematically organized or studied their lives.

Research shows that the two most reliable sources for their biographies are Complete Collection of Qing Officials’ Career Records and the Qi Family Genealogy of Yitong. The former contains career accounts written by the brothers themselves and is extremely useful for studying their official service in the late Qing. The latter, compiled by Qi Yaolin, supplements the record of their careers during the Republican period. While investigating these materials in April 2010, the author was fortunate to receive help from Ms. Qi Zongmin, a descendant of the brothers, who supplied fairly detailed biographical leads in a letter.

Another account appears in Yitong Historical and Cultural Materials, quoting the Yitong Local Gazetteer. That passage, however, incorrectly identifies the Qi brothers as Manchus and contains errors in several dates, so it is not sufficiently reliable. Other information is scattered through local gazetteers, Republican-era newspapers such as Shen Bao, and modern provincial reference compilations such as Materials on Conditions in Shandong Province, but these sources are neither detailed nor systematic. Appointment and dismissal orders in the Republican-era Government Gazette supplement information about the brothers’ official positions and can also be checked against the genealogy compiled by Qi Yaolin.

Taken together, the surviving materials now permit a general view of the brothers’ lives. Brief chronological biographies of Qi Yaolin and Qi Yaoshan follow.

III

The Harvard-Yenching Library’s Qi Brothers Collection does not consist of the brothers’ own writings, and no material has yet been found that explicitly explains the origins of their library. Preliminary research suggests that this batch is the most complete and substantial surviving portion of the brothers’ known collection. No catalog has yet been found showing that another institution or private individual possesses a comparable group of their books.¹

Viewed as a whole, the editions in the collection date mostly from the late Qing and early Republican periods. Most noteworthy is the fact that only two items are printed editions from the Guangxu or Xuantong reigns; all the rest are authorial manuscripts, manuscript copies, hand-drawn maps, waterway charts, and similar materials. Such a high proportion of manuscripts is rare even in a private collection. Thirty items are clearly authorial manuscripts, more than three hundred are Qing manuscript copies, and more than sixty are early Republican copies. Among the Qing copies whose dates can presently be established, the earliest comes from the Jiaqing reign, while most of the others cluster in the late Qing, between 1821 and 1911.

By content, the collection includes all four traditional bibliographic divisions: Classics, History, Masters, and Literary Collections. Using the classification standard of the Detailed Index to the Comprehensive Catalog of Chinese Collectanea, the author further divided the holdings as follows:

  • Classics: 19 works, covering the Book of Changes, Book of Documents, Book of Songs, Rites of Zhou, Book of Rites, Zuo Tradition to the Spring and Autumn Annals, Four Books, general discussions of the classics, and philology—nine subcategories in all. Most are manuscript copies of common works.
  • History: 152 works, covering standard histories, annalistic histories, miscellaneous histories, historical excerpts, historical criticism, biography, institutional works, calendars and seasonal observances, geography, and epigraphy—ten subcategories. Many concern local finance, statutes and precedents, memorials, official correspondence, and registers of officials.
  • Masters: 82 works, covering military texts, Daoism, allusions, Buddhism, crafts, calendrical science and mathematics, Confucian writings, occult arts, fiction, medicine, art, miscellaneous learning, and the philosophers of the Zhou and Qin—thirteen subcategories. The strongest concentrations are medicine, military technology, crafts, and trade.
  • Literary Collections: 160 works, covering individual collections, general anthologies, poetry and prose criticism, and lyric verse and drama—four subcategories. Many are musical scores, literary anthologies, and academy examination papers.

From these two aspects—the dating and physical character of the editions—it may be inferred that the collection was deliberately accumulated over several decades. Yet no ownership mark left by either brother has been found: no private seal, collector’s colophon, or inscription. A fuller investigation of how the collection was formed must therefore combine analysis of the specific contents with the brothers’ lives, careers, and personal associations.

For later readers of the Book of Changes and the Zuo Tradition, the two works may be called gateways to scholarship.

II. History

Works in the History division account for roughly one-third of the Qi Brothers Collection and constitute its most distinctive component. In addition to some excerpts copied from the standard histories and miscellaneous historical writings, the items most deserving attention are original files of local governments from the late Qing and early Republic: official registers, tax documents, trade records, accounts of government disbursements, statutes and precedents, memorials, and official correspondence. Because the Republican-period files have already been reproduced in other series, only works dating from the Qing are introduced here.

The official registers include Register of Commissioned Junior Officers of the Yellow River Encampment, Register of Expectant Civil Officials, Register of Military Officers in Bannered Garrisons, Register of Civil Officials of the Grand Canal Administration, and Register of Capital Officials, Prefects, Subprefects, Assistant Prefects, Magistrates Selected Through the Grand Selection, Assistant Department Magistrates, Department Judges, and Other Officials.

For local finance, there are documents known as “handover verification registers.” More than twenty were selected for facsimile reproduction, including Record of the Verification of a Single-Office Handover Register Compiled in Rugao County During the Guangxu Reign and Queries on the Handover Register of Baoshan County During the Guangxu Reign. Such registers were documents used when one local administration handed office over to its successor in the Ming and Qing. In the Qi Brothers Collection they concern mainly Jiangsu and Zhejiang, matching the regions in which the brothers served. Most consist of one fascicle, occasionally two or more, and most are red-ruled manuscript copies with seven to eleven lines per page. An official seal often appears at the beginning. Late-Qing seals are bilingual in Manchu and Chinese, whereas Republican-period seals are Chinese only. A typical opening title states that the incumbent official of a named place had investigated the term of a particular predecessor, from the year that predecessor assumed office through the day before he handed it over, and had prepared a verification register itemizing all regular and miscellaneous funds to be transferred, replenished, offset, reduced, or deleted. Ink corrections and transposition marks show that these were not final fair copies. The registers form an essentially continuous chronological series and are concentrated geographically. They are consequently rare first-hand materials for the economic history of Jiangsu and Zhejiang in the late Qing.

Two trade-related works deserve mention: Essential Knowledge for Trade and What One Must Know About the Pawn Business. The first, one fascicle in one case, was compiled by Wang Bingyuan and is followed by A Secret Record of Ingenious Conjuring Tricks. Wang lived during the Qianlong reign. The second, also one fascicle in one case, was compiled by the Weishan Hall of Xin’an, Zhejiang, toward the end of the Qing. Because it mentions events in the fifth year of the Xianfeng reign, it must have been completed after that date. Its language is concise, clear, rhythmic, and engaging. Such popular texts originated and circulated among ordinary people. Although no formally printed edition is known, they have considerable value for the study of popular customs.

Documents concerning the receipt and expenditure of government funds include Account Book of Disbursements by the Tianjin Telegraph Bureau During the Guangxu Reign, Expense Ledgers of Academies, Temples, and Other Institutions in Jiangsu Counties at the End of the Qing, and Reimbursement Registers of the Jinling General Bureau of Military Supplies. These illuminate late-Qing military expenditures and the operation of county academies, temples, and other institutions in Jiangsu.

There are more than ten works on statutes and precedents, mostly Qing legal provisions and selected copies of adjudicated cases from various places. Examples include Memorials Concerning Sichuan Bandits, Autumn-Assizes Regulations of the Daoguang Reign and Selected Provincial Cases, Copies of Sichuan Criminal Cases, Complete Autumn Case Files of Dongliu County for Republican Year 6, and Collected Judicial Records of Anhui Province.

The History division of the Qi Brothers Collection contains Waterway Maps of the Guangxu Reign: fifty-one hand-drawn sheets in excellent condition, some in black and white and others in color. Some maps are inscribed as having been drawn on a particular date in Guangxu 30 (1904), and some bear the official seals of local offices or responsible commissioners. In addition to waterway topography, they mark lijin tax stations.

These stations were local offices established by the Qing government after the Xianfeng reign to collect the lijin transit tax. The tax was levied at checkpoints on goods moving along major domestic land and water routes during the late Qing; it was generally one percent of the goods’ value.

The waterway and lijin-station maps in the Qi Brothers Collection were carefully drawn by local tax bureaus. They record the courses of waterways, the locations of the various tax bureaus, and their distances from provincial capitals. The sheets vary in size, showing that no standard format governed mapmaking in these offices. Some explanations are written on red labels pasted to the maps to attract the reader’s attention. Most depict rivers in Jiangxi; investigation shows that they cover eighty percent of the province’s river system. The collection also includes a map of lijin stations in Fengshan County, Taiwan. These constitute first-hand sources for late-Qing local hydraulic and economic history.

III. Masters

The Masters division contains many works on craft and technology, several of which deserve attention. Books on machinery and manufacturing include Complete Book of Craft Manufacture, Lessons on Naval Mines, Questions and Answers on Making Land Mines, with Questions and Answers on Making Batteries appended, and Illustrated Explanation of Aircraft. Many contain detailed manufacturing flowcharts or diagrams of product construction. The copyists’ work is highly accomplished, with elegant writing and meticulous illustrations, while the contents display clear characteristics of their period.

Other works concern everyday commercial goods, including Manual of Jade and Furs, Manual of Silver Currency and Jewelry, and An Exacting Search for the Most Precious Objects. Their authors were probably pawnshop owners active between the late Qing and early Republic, making the books rare sources for the study of the modern Chinese pawn trade.

The “Secrets of Jewelry” section in An Exacting Search for the Most Precious Objects and the “Discussion of Jewelry” in Manual of Silver Currency and Jewelry contain the same material, although certain phrases and the order of sections differ. The Masters division also contains works of calligraphy and painting and books of refined amusements, reflecting their owner’s cultivated tastes. One item, Copying the Authentic Calligraphy of Dong Wenmin, was originally cataloged as an authentic work; because its authenticity later became disputed, the words “copying” were added. Specialists are invited to settle the question.

IV. Literary Collections

Works in the Literary Collections division also make up roughly one-third of the Qi Brothers Collection. Particularly valuable are several individual Qing collections not cataloged elsewhere and a large number of dramatic and musical scores.

Among the individual collections are five works by the late-Qing author Cheng Zuo: Poems Preserved from Mingqiu Studio, Surviving Drafts from the Red-Sheep Calamity, Idle Chants from the Cold Office at Qiupu, The Sound of Cicadas in the Autumn Trees, and Chants from Worn-Broom Studio; and Zhu Zhongxuan’s Poems from Dandan Studio. None is cataloged by another holding institution. All were reproduced in Collected Ming and Qing Literary Works Held by the Harvard-Yenching Library, so they are not discussed further here. Also worthy of attention are Poems from Chuhui Studio, Poetry Drafts from Aiju Studio, and Poetry Manuscripts from Sanlun Study, none of which appears in the General Catalog of Individual Qing Collections or in other institutional catalogs.²

It has double borders on all four sides, an unprinted center margin, and a single yellow fishtail. “Hengqin Caotang” is printed at the bottom of the block center. The frame measures 15.8 × 10.2 cm. A pasted title slip appears at the end. The final title is “Written by Erqing, Master of Huanfengxuan.”

The Sound of Cicadas in the Autumn Trees comprises three fascicles and is a Hengqin Caotang manuscript-format copy from the late Qing (1885–1911). It has eight lines of twenty-one characters; the number of small characters per double line varies. It has double borders, an unprinted center margin, and a single yellow fishtail. “Hengqin Caotang” is printed at the bottom of the block center. The frame measures 15.6 × 10.2 cm. A green-ruled sheet occurs at the end of fascicle three; its frame measures 15.7 × 10.2 cm. The work was bound by gathering folded sheets into fascicles. A title slip is pasted at the end. Cheng Zuo’s original manuscript had four fascicles, but only three survive. The title slip bears “sealed above a stone table.” A poem in the book, “Joy at the Rain,” was composed in Guangxu 11 (1885). The volume ends with poems written in response to Zhang Wenhe in Guangxu 8 (1882), again indicating that Cheng Zuo was then in office. It bears the seals “Cheng Zuo” and “Erqing.”

Chants from Worn-Broom Studio is one fascicle, a Hengqin Caotang manuscript-format copy from the late Qing (1881–1911). It has eight lines per half-page with no fixed character count, double borders, an unprinted center margin, and a single yellow fishtail. “Hengqin Caotang” appears at the bottom of the block center. The frame measures 15.7 × 10.1 cm. A title slip is pasted at the end. The contents comprise four juan of poetry in the following order: poems written in response to Zhang Wenhe in Guangxu 3 (1877), poems on historical subjects from Guangxu 5 (1879), and poems composed at the Yamen of Li Shixian in Guangxu 7 (1881). The final title reads “Written by Erqing, Master of Huanfengxuan.” It bears a long rectangular relief seal reading “A Book for Reading in Leisure.”

All five works above are authorial fair copies. None appears in the General Catalog of Individual Qing Collections. The collection also contains several other previously uncataloged works:

Poems from Chuhui Studio, by the Qing author Fu Xinbo, is one roughly bound manuscript fascicle from the late Qing (1887–1911), with eight lines of twenty-two characters and no ruling. The title appears at the beginning, followed by “Written by Fu Xinbo, Former Recluse of Mochou Lake.” The latest cyclical date in the fascicle is dinghai. A poem title states that only a few decades had passed “from the Xianfeng and Tongzhi reigns to the present,” showing that this dinghai year was Guangxu 13 (1887) and that the book was completed afterward.

Poetry Drafts from Aiju Studio, by the Qing author Li Shixi, is one Leyutang manuscript fascicle from the late Qing (1868–1911). It has eight lines of twenty characters; small characters follow the same single-line arrangement, and there is no ruling. It is bound in the “gold-inlaid-jade” style and contains red-ink circles. “Leyutang” is written horizontally on the cover label. The volume begins with the author’s preface dated Daoguang 28 (1848), ending: “All punctuation and criticism in these poems follows the written comments of Mr. Zhang, the distinguished judge of talent known as Sheng’an.” An author’s postscript dated Xianfeng 3 (1853) appears at the end. The table of contents bears the title Newly Revised Poetry Drafts from Aiju Studio and concludes: “Respectfully copied by Su Yungong beneath the north wall of Shuiyue Study in the first ten days of the third month, Tongzhi 7 (1868).”

Poetry Manuscripts from Sanlun Study, by the Qing author Xu Yintao, is one authorial manuscript fascicle dated Guangxu 19 (1893). It has six lines of eighteen characters; small characters appear in double lines of varying length, with no ruling. It contains ink corrections and transposition marks, marginal comments, and red circles. A long cover inscription states that the fascicle contains seventy mock examination poems composed for others, seven by the author himself, eight copied from earlier writers, and one additional regulated verse; it also says that later compositions were entered separately. Other inscriptions read “Draft manuscript of Xu Yintao, the Student Who Listens to Autumn” and “Begun in the first ten days of the second month of the guisi year, Guangxu [1893]; read by the Old Man of Fu’an Village, born on the Double Seventh.” Because the paper is damaged, only “Sanlun Study” survives from the title on the cover; “Poetry Manuscripts” has been supplied from the contents. The book bears six seals: “Listening to Autumn,” “Yintao,” “Refinement Does Not Depend on Being Known,” “Made After the Age of Eighty,” “The Ageless Old Man Born on the Double Seventh,” and “Born on the Double Seventh.”

These writers and their books have become known to the world only because the Qi Brothers Collection was made public.¹

The Literary Collections division also contains more than thirty dramatic scores with gongche notation; some even include percussion notation. It is exceptionally rare for one family to have assembled so many such scores. Yang Yinliu wrote in A Short Introduction to Gongche Notation: “Gongche notation is a form of musical notation widely used among the Chinese people. It has been used among the people for at least a thousand years, since the Tang dynasty. Yet even within gongche notation, its specific application differs by period, region, and musical genre.”² All of the gongche scores in the Qi Brothers Collection have been reproduced in facsimile. Further research is needed to determine the theatrical genres to which they belong.

Another distinctive group in the Literary Collections division consists of late-Qing examination essays from academies: Examination Papers of Zunjing Academy, Examination Papers of Wenzheng Academy, Examination Papers of Zhongshan Academy, and compilations such as Academy Essays, Classes A and B and Qing Academy Examination Papers. The Zunjing, Wenzheng, and Zhongshan academies were all in Jiangning.³ Their examination paper was similar in format: red-ruled, with six lines of twenty-five characters. The paper used by Zunjing and Wenzheng academies bears the clear printer’s brand “Tongrenyu.” Each student’s work occupies a separate fascicle. The cover of each gives the date and names the county magistrate who graded it. These documents not only preserve late-Qing academy examination prose for reading; they also retain original traces of every stage—setting questions, grading, and fair-copying. They are first-hand evidence for studying late-Qing academy administration and its relationship with state schools.

IV

The Qi Brothers Collection at the Harvard-Yenching Library is rich in content and deserves serious consideration for the value it offers research and practical use.

When Wang Zhongmin evaluated the collection in February 1945, he offered this preliminary judgment:

“On the whole, the copying is crude and the contents are lowbrow. Viewed from another angle, however, it has a different kind of value. There are fifty or sixty dramatic scripts, all annotated in gongche characters; these were plainly textbooks used by theatrical troupes to train apprentices. There are also county magistrates’ handover registers, running accounts from county-gazetteer bureaus, and academy examination papers—all of which, several centuries from now, will surely become historical materials of the greatest importance. Pawnshops and native banks, moreover, are distinctive institutions within Chinese commerce.… If this batch of books is carefully preserved abroad, will it not become another set of Dunhuang manuscripts several centuries hence?”¹

Wang’s assessment can be understood in two ways. From the perspective of traditional bibliography and edition studies, the Qi Brothers Collection falls far short of the standard for “rare books.” Placed in a broader historical context, however, it possesses distinctive documentary value.

Compiling this collection gave us an opportunity to engage deeply with the Qi Brothers Collection. After a preliminary reading of the documents, its value may be summarized in six respects:

  1. First-hand material for studying the lives and associations of the Qing father and son Xue Huan and Xue Huapei, and of the brothers Qi Yaolin and Qi Yaoshan across the dynastic transition. The collection was accumulated during the careers of two generations of officials from the late Qing through the early Republic. Every book accompanied its owner closely and can reveal information about him.

  2. Important historical material on Chinese politics, economics, military affairs, culture, and other fields from the late Qing to the early Republic. The collection bears characteristics imparted by its owners’ political careers and lives, and the events in which they participated were often historically significant. Moving from the particular to the general, study of these materials can help reconstruct nearly a century of history from late-Qing China through the early Republic.

  3. Rich evidence of social life among scholars, farmers, artisans, and merchants from the late Qing to the early Republic. Many items concern the operation and organization of government agencies at various levels, educational institutions, and economic organizations, offering a relatively comprehensive picture of society at the time.

  4. Vivid records of the personal lives of local officials, secretarial staff, and ordinary students from the late Qing to the early Republic. The collection preserves a considerable quantity of personal information: local officials’ self-education, professional training for secretarial staff, and the academy life of ordinary students all convey a vivid sense of lived experience.

  5. A supplement to the literary and artistic record of China from the late Qing to the early Republic. The personal literary collections and materials on drama and music provide an excellent supplement to the surviving literature and art of this turbulent period.

In 1945, writing to Arthur William Hummel, chief of the Library of Congress’s Division of Orientalia, about the Qi Brothers Collection, Kaiming Chiu lamented:

“As a matter of fact, as a Chinese, I really do not care who buys these books—whether the Library of Congress or the Chinese-Japanese Library of the Harvard-Yenching Institute—because it remains an enormous loss to China.”¹

Today, more than seventy years later, the Harvard-Yenching Library and National Library Press have collaborated to reproduce the Qi Brothers Collection and bring it once again before the world. It is hoped that more scholars will study these works and discover their value. Perhaps this may offer some small consolation to the librarians of an earlier generation.
